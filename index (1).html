Social & Emotional Learning from the Start

Children build social and emotional skills through relationships, responsive care, language, play, routines, and opportunities to practice. Educators can intentionally create environments that help children recognize feelings, connect with others, manage behavior, and develop confidence.

Why Social & Emotional Learning Matters

Social-emotional development is not separate from learning—it supports children's ability to participate, communicate, form relationships, cope with stress, and engage in classroom activities. Palmer (2019) emphasizes that responsive relationships and high-quality early learning settings are central to this development. Educators support it through warm interactions, culturally responsive practices, and everyday opportunities to communicate, explore, and learn.

From pregnancy to adolescence

Prenatal health and caregiving conditions can influence the developing nervous system and later regulation. After birth, responsive relationships, predictable routines, language-rich interactions, and safe opportunities to explore help children develop attachment, emotional understanding, and behavioral regulation.

Early adversity does not determine a child's future. Supportive relationships and early intervention can buffer stress and create opportunities for healthier development. Research describes early experiences as important influences on developing brain architecture and later learning, behavior, and health (Shonkoff et al., 2012).

Why educators matter

Teachers provide repeated opportunities for children to practice social problem-solving, identify emotions, wait, take turns, communicate needs, and recover from frustration. A warm, predictable classroom can function as an important developmental context.

Self-regulation develops through interacting biological, behavioral, and environmental processes; it supports engagement in learning and school adjustment (Blair & Raver, 2015).

Relationships build securityWarm, responsive interactions help children feel safe enough to explore, communicate, and learn.
Skills grow with practiceAdults can model emotion words, calm breathing, and problem-solving before difficult moments arise.
Key message: Development is dynamic. Milestones describe common patterns, not rigid deadlines. Observe the individual child, consider context, and collaborate with families and appropriate professionals when concerns persist.

Three Developmental Stages

The examples below focus on prenatal development, infancy, and early childhood. Milestones should be interpreted within the child's culture, relationships, health, and individual developmental pattern.

01
🌱

Prenatal Development

Social/emotional foundations:

  • Development of the fetal nervous system and stress-response systems.
  • Pregnancy health and caregiver well-being can influence the prenatal environment.
  • Protective factors include appropriate prenatal care, nutrition, health support, and reduced exposure to harmful stressors.

Classroom connection

Teachers do not “teach” prenatal milestones. Their role begins by understanding that prenatal conditions may contribute to later developmental needs and by using strengths-based, family-centered practices after birth.

02
🤝

Infancy

Common social/emotional milestones:

  • Builds attachment through responsive interactions with familiar caregivers.
  • Uses facial expressions, sounds, gestures, and movement to communicate needs.
  • Shows increasing interest in social interaction and familiar people.
  • Begins to use adult support to calm and organize emotions.

Classroom connection

Responsive caregiving, predictable routines, face-to-face interaction, and shared attention help infants develop trust and early regulation. Honig and Wittmer (2017) also recommend comforting distressed infants and toddlers, using gentle care, and giving children words for their feelings.

03
🧩

Early Childhood

Common social/emotional milestones:

  • Names or communicates basic emotions with increasing accuracy.
  • Begins to use simple strategies to manage frustration with adult support.
  • Participates in cooperative play and practices sharing and turn-taking.
  • Develops empathy and begins to consider how actions affect others.

Classroom connection

Play, emotion coaching, classroom routines, and guided peer problem-solving create daily practice for social-emotional skills. Adults can validate feelings while setting clear, safe limits and teaching children what they can do next.

Classroom Strategies: Two for Each Stage

🌱 Prenatal: Strategy 1 — Family partnership

Use welcoming, nonjudgmental communication with families. Share information about available prenatal and family-support resources without making assumptions about a family's circumstances.

🌱 Prenatal: Strategy 2 — Strengths-based transition planning

When supporting expectant families or planning for a child entering an early-learning program, gather family priorities and strengths. Coordinate with appropriate professionals when developmental or health concerns affect classroom participation.

🤝 Infancy: Strategy 1 — Serve-and-return interactions

Notice an infant's gaze, sound, facial expression, or movement; respond promptly with words, facial expressions, and shared attention. Pause so the infant has an opportunity to respond again.

🤝 Infancy: Strategy 2 — Emotion-coaching routines

During feeding, transitions, or moments of distress, use a calm voice and simple language: “You are upset. I am here. Let's take a slow breath and get comfortable.” Pair words with predictable actions.

🧩 Early childhood: Strategy 1 — Feelings check-in

Begin the day with an emotion board. Children can point to, name, or draw how they feel. Model that different feelings are acceptable and teach safe ways to respond.

🧩 Early childhood: Strategy 2 — Problem-solving play

Use puppets or role-play to present everyday conflicts such as sharing materials or joining a game. Ask children to identify the feeling, describe the problem, and generate two safe solutions.

Understanding Self-Regulation

Self-regulation is the developing ability to manage attention, emotions, thoughts, and behavior in ways that support a goal or situation. Young children initially depend heavily on adults for co-regulation; with repeated practice and supportive relationships, they gradually internalize strategies.

Example 1: Waiting

A child wants a turn with a popular toy, feels frustrated, and uses a teacher-taught strategy—waiting with a timer or choosing another activity.

Example 2: Calming

A child notices strong feelings and uses a familiar routine such as breathing, asking for help, moving to a quiet space, or talking about the problem.

Example 3: Attention

A child follows a short sequence of directions, notices distraction, and returns attention to the task with an adult cue.

How teachers can model and encourage self-regulation

1. Think aloud

Model your own process: “I feel frustrated because the schedule changed. I am going to pause, take a breath, and look at our new plan.” This makes invisible regulation strategies visible.

2. Teach before the crisis

Practice breathing, movement breaks, asking for help, and emotion vocabulary when children are calm. Then cue the same strategies during challenging moments.

3. Use predictable routines

Visual schedules, transition warnings, consistent expectations, and calm spaces reduce unnecessary cognitive load and give children repeated opportunities to practice regulation.

Remember

Self-regulation is a developmental skill, not simply “good behavior.” Corrective responses should teach a replacement skill rather than shame a child for having an emotion.

Everyday Practices That Support Social-Emotional Growth

The assigned readings share a common message: children learn regulation and relationship skills through repeated experiences with attentive adults. These practices can be adapted to a child’s age, communication style, family context, and individual needs.

💬

Put feelings into words

Notice a child’s cues and name the emotion without judgment: “You look disappointed that the toy is being used.” Offer words, gestures, or picture choices so children can communicate in ways that work for them.

Informed by Honig and Wittmer (2017).

🫶

Comfort first, then teach

When a baby or toddler is distressed, stay calm, offer appropriate comfort, and reduce overwhelming stimulation. Once the child is calmer, reconnect and guide the next step. Young children often need adult co-regulation before they can use a strategy independently.

Informed by Honig and Wittmer (2017) and Slater (2021).

🌈

Make the classroom welcoming

Use predictable routines, inclusive books and play materials, and respectful communication with families. Learn about each child’s strengths, culture, language, and context instead of assuming one approach fits everyone.

Informed by Palmer (2019).

Respond to behavior as communication

Ask what a child may be communicating through behavior—such as fatigue, frustration, sensory overload, uncertainty, or a need for connection. Observe patterns, adjust the environment when possible, and explicitly teach a safe replacement skill. Avoid shaming or treating a developing skill as a character flaw.

Partner with families and specialists

Share specific observations, invite family perspectives, and agree on consistent supports where practical. If concerns persist or affect participation, collaborate with the family and appropriate developmental or mental-health professionals. Seek help early rather than relying on punishment alone.

Try it tomorrow: Choose one routine—arrival, cleanup, snack, or turn-taking. Model the skill, use the same simple words consistently, notice children’s attempts, and give them time to practice.

Family Partnership: Learning Continues at Home

Families can strengthen social-emotional development through ordinary routines. The goal is connection and practice—not perfection.

🌱 Prenatal

  • Attend recommended prenatal care appointments.
  • Discuss questions and concerns with qualified health professionals.
  • Build a support network and identify practical sources of help.

🤝 Infancy

  • Respond warmly to sounds, facial expressions, and gestures.
  • Use songs, talking, reading, and face-to-face play.
  • Keep daily routines as predictable as practical.
  • Comfort and label emotions: “You are tired. I am here.”

🧩 Early childhood

  • Name emotions during everyday events.
  • Practice calming strategies when the child is already calm.
  • Give two appropriate choices to support independence.
  • Read stories and discuss characters' feelings and possible solutions.
  • Praise specific regulation: “You waited for your turn even though it was hard.”
Family message: If a child consistently struggles with communication, relationships, emotional regulation, or daily functioning, share observations with the child's teacher and healthcare/developmental professionals. Early support can make a meaningful difference.
